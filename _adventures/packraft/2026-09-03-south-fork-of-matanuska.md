## Context

Shortly after fiance Mary made plans to spend a summer in Palmer working as a public defender, I booked flights to vist her for the last two weeks of her summer. We both planned to take that second week completely off from work and get out for some Alaska adventures. As I initially began reading up on Alaska adventures I found Luc Mehl’s writeup of his [traverse across the Brooks Range](https://thingstolucat.com/brooks-range-traverse/). We did not have the time or the experience for such an epic route, but a packrafting trip (if weather cooperated) was in the cards. Our constraints: 

The whitewater needed to not exceed Class II. This would be our first time in Packraft and first time on rivers in Alaska. While Mary and I have both spent some time on class III whitewater in kayaks and canoes, there were enough variables with this trip I did not want to add additional whitewater risk. 
The trip needed to be reasonably accessible from Palmer, AK where Mary was living. With only a week to work with we did not want to spend too much time driving or flying. 

With these two constraints I found [this route](https://thingstolucat.com/2021/09/24/south-fork-matanuska-loop/) on Luc’s website. He described the route as “ This has got to be a candidate for the best true packraft loop around, especially during the autumn”. 
The best true packraft loop in the autumn sounded pretty good to me. Our dates were early September which lined up well to have some fall colors and not too much water. Now we just had to hope for good weather. 

Once I arrived in Alaska, we began filling in some of the details. Prior to the packrafting trip, we made plans to fish on the Kenia river with some of Mary’s coworkers. We would rent packrafts and dry suites as we were driving back through Anchorage from [The Hoarding Marmot](https://hoardingmarmot.com/) on Tuesday, September 1st and return them right before we flew out on Sunday September 6th. That gave us four days from Wednesday to Saturady to complete the loop. 

Once the weather forecast was near our decision became clear: Wednesday through Friday were forecasted to be decently clear and Saturady was forecasted to be rainy. So we would hike on Wednesday morning and plan to exit on Friday.

Our friend Nahla had packrafted this specific route the previous year, and offered the following beta

There's one spot where there's like a really specific descent down this cliffside and if you don't find it, it looks really really impossible. There is a lot of bush whacking and I think the way that we went was not the most optimal way and so I'd recommend a different way. Those are the two main things and then just knowing and being prepared for the water is continuous class II so it's not intense but for my friend it was a little intimidating just because it does feel like if you were to flip it would be really easy to float down river. Dry suits are absolutely necessary. That's pretty much it. We saw a lot of bear scat, but no bears, no moose. Yeah, it was incredible truly

Nahla was kind enough to send us her GPX which provided slightly more detail then the route that Luc had marked up on Google Maps. 

## Day 1

We anticipated that the weather might begin to deteriorate Friday afternoon and this trip held a decent amount of uncertainty so we decided to get an early start. I set my alarm for 4:45 and we departed Palmer at about 5:15AM and drove most of the way to the Lionhead trailhead in the dark. 

It was light by the time we made it to the trailhead. It's about ¾ of a mile walk to Cariboo Creek from the trailhead. There we began inflating our boats for the second time (the first time was at The Hoarding Marmot the previous afternoon). Immediately I was concerned that the valve on my packraft did not seem to be operating correctly as a one way valve despite being turned in the “left” direction. However, after brief consultation with the web (we still had service) we learned that Alpacca Raft does not intend their valves to be perfect one way valves and the airflow out only stops once the cap is tight tightened around the valve after filling the boat fully. 

With added confidence from this fact we completed inflating the packrafts. I was using the Classic model while Mary was on a Gnarwhal. The Gnarwhal includes a zipper that allows gear to be stored in the pack raft tubing. Very clever! The Classic does not have that feature, so I gave Mary most of my stuff and then had my backpack sit between my legs. 

The first packraft section is about a mile and involves floating down Cariboo Creek until it confluences with the Matanuska and then taking out on the far side of the Matanuska. We put in right as the first rays of sun were hiting the river. The packraft turned out to be pretty straightforward. When we took out on the river-left of the Matanuska, the air temp must have been below freezing because ice formed on our boat and gear as we were packing up. As we started towards Spoon Valley initially the trail was pretty good, but later on it disappeared. Nahla’s track that we were following hugged the creek, but in interest of keeping our feet dry we stayed further right and ended up having a bit of a bush whack and additional climb that may or may not have been better than getting our feet wet.

We stayed as far left up against the cliff as possible as we headed further up Spoon Valley. The first crux was a narrow creek section that had cascading falls coming down. It was still early enough in the morning that the rocks above water level had a sheen of ice on them making everything very slippery. We had to cross the cascading creek right before one falls and after another. The crossing itself was simple enough and the water was never more than a foot deep but the consequences (getting washed down the lower falls) felt a bit dire. I went first and decided the footing below the water line was pretty decent. I ferried both our packs across and then Mary followed. There were not any additional challenging moves as we climbed up the canyon, but even with the dry suits our feet became reasonably cold. 

![](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06338.jpeg)
*Chilly!*

Once out of the canyon, we stopped to warm up in the sun and eat a snack. Would we continue to be walking in creek beds as we walked up Spoon Valley?

As we approached the next side slot I could see from a histance that it was a dry creek slot with no water running down it. Happy days!

From there we had an easy walk up the rest of the valley. The colors were beginning to change and the walking was an easy gradient. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06351.jpeg)
*The lower sections of Matanuska Glacier could bennefit from a wash.*

Reviewing Nahla’s map we concluded the next crux would be decending into the valley we would turn hike up into off Spoon Valley. 

Indeed it was the case. The route Nahla took appeared to be a cliff. Perhaps with closer inspection would have shown us a viable route but we decided instead to continue climbing and descend down a slope with lower average slope angles. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06362.jpeg)
*Looking down into the valley we hope to ascend. So close and yet so far away.*

We ultimately did indeed find a decent trail down that slope but spent about 1.5 hours traversing less than a mile of distance. 

We decided we would walk another four miles or so until the route branched off the current river valley we where in and than camp there. That four miles went by pretty quickly. We crossed the river a handleful of times but the warmer air temps made getting our feet wet not nearly as unplesant as it had been that morning. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06378.jpeg){: width="50%" .align-center }

We made it to our camp spot a little bit before 7, pitched the tent, organized our gear, ate our burritos purchased from the only Mexican restraunte in Palmer (not Taco Bell this time) a distance from camp, put all our food in a bear bin and stashed it in a bush, and got into the tent. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06382.jpeg)

Temps dropped pretty quickly once the sun went down (maybe cold air blowing down from the glacier at the head of the valley?). I was very happy to have brought a 0 degree Marmot bag that I recently inherited from my dad. Mary was in a 15 degree bag, but with a sleeping bag liner and additional layers she also claimed to be sufficently warm. 

Mary set her alarm for 6:30 (my watch was already dead after attempting to record our 12 hour hike) and we feel alssep at about 9. 

## Day 2
On waking, I ideally wondered how frozen our trailrunners would be. They where still wet from the creek crossing when we took them off. We had been smart and loosened them as much as possibly so that we’d still be able to put them on even if they really where quite frozen. Fortunately, whatever the temperature in the vestibule of the tent it was not that far below freezing. Our shoes where slighly frozen but still quite easy to put on. 

We packed up the gear, ate a snack, and started walking. The previous night we use CalTopo’s handy feature that predicts which slopes will be exposed to sun at a specific time to see were we would see our first sunlight, because we where hiking up a most west facing slope we likely would not get sun until the pass and the high point of the trip. We would eat breakfast there. 

The creek walking was mostly fine. There was snow on the ground in places which made the rocks a little slippery but otherwise the walking was pretty good. When we had the chance we cut up to the left of the creek and walked on more southerly slopes that were largely clear of snow. Periodically we would see bear prints. The big fella seemed to be going the same place we were. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06400.jpeg)

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06406.jpeg)

The final challenge before getting to the pass was to walk up a small creek that is steep in places. Fortunately the creek was laregely south facing with minimal snow in the actual creek bed. All the same, we where happy to be hiking up the creek rather then down it. 

The snow on the ground increased once we finished the steep part of the climb and leved out to make the final ascent to the pass. Fortunately there where foot prints leading over the pass. On close inspection we discovered it was the same bear as earlier. His prints must have been from a day or two prior because they had had the change to melt in the sun and left us with a nice trail to follow. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06416.jpeg)
*Mary follows the track of our friend the bear.*

We ate breakfast and took some photos on the top of the pass. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06440.jpeg)
*A gentlement always carries the life jackets as they say.*

The first part of the descent from the pass was also pretty straight forward. We walked down a dry ravine until it eventually became a creek and than walked down the creek until there was an easy option to walk parallel to the creek on the left hand side. Nahla had walked on the right side, but today there was about 6 inches of snow on that side. The final moves to rejoin the creek after our high line to the left where a little steep and dicey but we where happy with our route. 

After rejoining the creek we than ascended about 400 feet up to a small knowl which seems like a lovely camp location with a view down towards the South Fork of the Matanuska. Looking down into the valley we heard and engine start and saw a small bush plane take flight far below. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06473.jpeg)
*Rarely do I find myself so far above a flying plane.*

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06486.jpeg)
*That looks like a river we can paddle!*

We ate lunch. I guessed from here we would make it to the river by 2:30 PM. The river just wasn’t that far away. About a mile and 1500 feet of descending. My prediction turned out to be very optimistic. There was enough slippery snow in the main gully we descended we tried to traverse towards greener pastures. However the green pastures turned out to be the worse bush whack Mary and I have ever had the good fortune to fine ourselves in. 

We started the descent shouting “Hey Bear” ever once and a while in case the big fella we followed across the pass has decided to park himself on this hillside. However, as the brush gets thicker we stop calling assuming that no self respecting bear would get himself into such a dense thicket. 

At one point we get close to a small creek so I think: What the heck, maybe the the creek is a bit more clear and we can walk down it. However, the bushes and tight trees start to be pretty bad as we get close to the creek. Maybe we should turn around, I say to Mary. How much further she asks. 39 feet I say. Lets go for it she says. So we do but damed if it doesn’t take us 15 minutes to cover those 39 feet and the creek it turns out actually has even tighter trees then not the creek, so we spend another 15 minutes beating our way back to our starting location on a slightly different heading. 

Ahh well. We do eventually make it to the river. We get there at 4pm and have a snack and water to celebrate the occasion. From our snack location we walk downstream for about 10 minutes until the creek we where on earlier joins the South Fork. There we stop, inflate our packrafts, and prepare to push off. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06509.jpeg)
*What beautiful contraptions these Alpaca Rafts are!*

Of all the various challenges this trip poses, the one we had focused on the most  prior to the trip was this class II descent. Neither Mary nor I have ever pack rafted. Would we be able to handle a class II river in a packraft? Even with drysuits swimming stuck both of us as a very serious and largely unacceptable outcome. The short float at the start of the trip had bolstered our confidence and looking at the South Fork further inspired us. For one thing, there isn’t much water in the river. It seemed like the biggest risk would be getting stuck in places were the braided river diverged. 

We started floating and pretty quickly realized that this float was going to work out, so long as we didn’t come across any rapids of which we had not found any reports nor been able to spot any very big features with satellite footage. 

We floated for about and hour and half and made camp on the river plane on an elevated gravel bar next to the river.

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06537.jpeg)

Our camp caught the last rays of sun and we ate a very plesant dinner of ramen and instant potatoes reveling in the feeling of overcoming so many challenges on this trip that at times felt like they might be insurmountable. 

Compared with the previous night the weather was warm and we seemingly had the whole South Fork of the Matanuska valley all to ourselves.

## Day 3 

Today we have no urgency to get moving. The sun will not hit our spot in the valley until 9:15 or so. We sleep into 7:00 or so and than I go recover the bear can from where we had stashed it the night before and heat water for oats. After a chill breakfast, we pack up camp and inflate our boats (Mary had read about some packraft beings punctured by a bear so we decided to deflate our the night before and store them in the vestibule of the tent). 

We hit the water at 9AM right as the first light is making its way into the valley. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06541.jpeg)

The largest challenge with our packraft setup is that my seat cushion has a leak and slowly deflates over about 15 minutes. This causes two problems. First, my butt is a bit unprotected when we smash into rocks which happens regularly with the low water levels (during most of the float a quick paddle poak shows the depth to be less then 12 inches). Second, I sit lower in the packraft then the designed intended so when water splashes onto my spray skirt  it forms a small and unplesant moat around my torso. I attempt to mitage the issue by sitting on top of my largely empty backpack. This helps. 

Otherwise, the packrafts perform great and we cover easy miles at a rapid pace. After many hiking miles that were painfully slow it feels great to be whizzing along with only the ocasional paddle stroke. 

We stop for a short snack once the river bends but otherwise float pretty continously. It is a beautiful day.

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06575.jpeg)

We take out well above were Caribou Creek intersects with the Matanuska and pack our packraft a bit sloppily and hike back towards the car. 

![alt text](/images/2026-09-south-fork-of-the-matanuska/DSC06580.jpeg)
*Take out.*


A short hike finds us back at the car. We are the only ones in the parking lot. Magical trip! 

## Notes
- Bring seweed + packet chicken for better flavor for ramen dinner. 
- We carried two lighters which was good because I got one of them wet and it stopped working. Maybe consier the MSR pizeo electric lighter? 
- We were limited by rental inventory, but in the future two Gnarwhals would be ideal for this trip so both boats can hold some of the gear. 
- Storing a backpack between ones legs or under ones butt results in that backpack getting totally soaked. 
- We should have broken our paddles down more than we did. We each just slipt the paddle into two sections rather than four. After each bush whack I convinced myself there would be no further bush whacks. And I was wrong except for the last time.
